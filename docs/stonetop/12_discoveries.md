**Discoveries**


**Discoveries** are the interesting things that
PCs encounter, stuff that isn’t immediately
dangerous but that prompts investigation,
interaction, and decisions. They include:



**Clues**, like a monster’s footprints in



the muddy soil
**Sites**, like the monster’s den
**Encounters**, like the curious spirit met



while following the monster’s tracks
**Opportunities**, like the eggs found in



the monster’s den
**Artifacts**, like the strange medallion



tucked in the nest with the eggs
**Arcana**, like the lime-caked runes



carved in the walls of the den, detailing
a spell that can hide you from death



Discoveries can also be **threats** (page
277) or **dangers** (page 379). An aurochs
quietly munching away is a discovery—a
chance to acquire meat and valuable horns!
But it’s also a monster, one that could really mess the PCs up if they aren’t careful.


Add discoveries to the game when…

… **a player move prompts you to** . When

a PC Seeks Insight and asks, “What
happened here recently?” your answer
might take the form of a clue. When
they Forage and pick, “You discover
something interesting or useful,” you’re
almost certainly going to present them
with a discovery of some sort.

… **your prep demands it** . If you’ve already

decided that there’s a particular artifact
buried in the pile of rubble, and the
PCs dig through that pile, then of
course they find the artifact.



These aren’t formal categories, and they
often overlap. Those footprints might be
a clue that the monster is nearby, but they
might also be an opportunity to track it
down or find its den.

you make up some bandits, and the

**Discoveries**


_Book II: The Wider World and Other Wonders_
contains all manner of discoveries for you
to use in your game. The entries for each
region and location include tables that you
can pick from or roll on. The entries for
the various peoples (like Green Lords) and
powers (like the Things Below) include
tables for sites and lists of potential artifacts and arcana. Use these tools as much
or as little as makes sense for you and your
game. **Exploit the setting guide!**

Use discoveries to tell stories, to **portray**
**a rich and mysterious world** . When you
prep a discovery or create one on the fly,
**think offscreen** . Think about how it came
to be here. Tie it to past events or ongoing
situations. Don’t just have the PCs meet
a merchant on the road, have them cross
paths with Ulna, the wheezy old merchant
who sold them that lantern. Don’t just have
them find some boars to hunt, have them
encounter (normally fearless) boars who
are fleeing in terror from the demon who’s
awoken deeper in the Wood.

Use discoveries to **punctuate their lives**
**with adventure** . Give them word of a
powerful artifact or an unexplored ruin, as
a way to prompt an expedition. While on
an expedition, present discoveries that the
PCs can wonder at, interact with, explore.
Reward their bravery and curiosity with yet
more discoveries.

Once you’ve put a discovery in the game,
follow the **core loop** (page 169). Ask the
PCs what they do, resolve their actions,
and repeat. Maybe they ignore a discovery
and move on. Maybe they take it with
them, or make plans to come back later.
Maybe the discovery snowballs into a
completely new situation. **Play to find out**
**what happens** .

**Clues**

Clues point the PCs towards some sort of
knowledge, conclusion, or revelation. They
are signifiers of something else. They are
nouns that tell stories.

Present clues when PCs Seek Insight, as a
way to **begin and end with the fiction** as
you answer their questions. Use them to
**announce trouble** or **hint at more than**
**meets the eye**, to **offer an opportunity**, or
to make any other GM move that involves
imparting knowledge or establishing facts.

**Creating clues**

If a provocative, juicy clue just occurs to
you, or something in _Book II_ suggests a
clue that resonates, then ask yourself
whether it contradicts your prep or any
established truths. If not, use it!

Otherwise: start with the **revelation**, the
thing you want the PCs to potentially
learn. Work backwards from there. Imagine a clue that could lead to the revelation,
such as:



Signs left on the environment (tracks,



crushed grass, an old firepit, etc.)
Physical remains (blood, bones, hair,



fur, scales, feathers, droppings, etc.)
Something out of place (a clean bowl



on a dusty table, a strange idol, etc.)
Writing, art, or markings (a note or



letter, a mosaic, idle graffiti, etc.)
An NPC’s/monster’s behavior (a ghost

pointing, a liar’s shifty eyes, etc.)
Noises, glimpses, scents (a howl in the

distance, smoke on the wind, etc.)
Rumors, reports, tales, or gossip (heard

from an NPC, remembered, etc.)
Flashes, omens, or revelations (a dream,



Some clues point directly towards their
revelation. Others merely hint at it, and
make sense only with some effort or in
hindsight. Present clues that fit in with the
established fiction, and **play to find out**
**what happens** .

If you’re stumped, maybe tell the players
the revelation and ask how they figure it
out. “What should you be on the lookout
for? Hmm. The hdour’s watching this place
via magic, and they know you’re here!
What do you notice that tells you this?”

**Prepping clues**

As you prep for a session, try to identify any
**crucial revelations** that the PCs need in order
to accomplish their current goals or to make
important decisions. For each revelation, create at least one clue, ideally two or three. The
players need this info to meaningfully engage
with the game, right? Make sure that there are
plenty of ways for them to get it.


_Vahid has dragged the PCs to Three_
_Coven Lake, in search of the Mindgem’s_
_fiery makerglass heart. He’s acquired an_
_ancient scroll that hints at it once being_
_here, powering some arcane device._

_In order to accomplish their goals, the_
_PCs will need to learn where exactly the_
_Mindgem’s heart is. I’ve decided that it’s_
_still powering that arcane device, which_
_animates a number of constructs. But a_
_deathless sorcerer long ago fused his soul_
_to that device, sending his consciousness_
_out into those constructs._

_I think about potential clues, ways that_
_the PCs could learn some or all of this._
_Then I write out the following:_


  - An old map of the city, showing a
site marked with a sigil that’s also
prominent on the scroll.

 - The constructs bear that same
sigil from the scroll; the PCs might
interrogate one or follow it home.

  - A rival of the sorcerer tells the PCs
where to find the arcane device,
hoping to stir up conflict.

**Sites**

Sites are interesting places that the PCs
might want to explore. There’s a whole
chapter devoted to **Sites** (page 345).

But sites can be discoveries, too! As the
PCs trek through the Steplands, they
might stumble on a Stone Lord ruin. If
they explore that ruin, there might be
smaller sites within—a shrine to Aratis,
perhaps, itself containing a Chronicle from
the days of the Makers. Sites within sites
within sites!

To create a site, or an area within one:

**1) Lay the foundation**
Ask questions
Build on what you’ve got
Exploit the setting guide

**2) Build up its story**
Look for connections
Ask yourself questions
Answer those questions
Create a timeline

**3) Sketch out the site’s contents**
Populate it
Identify dangers and discoveries
Establish areas/rooms
Describe the environment
Arrange areas/rooms

**4) Write it up as much as you like**
Create a map or visual (maybe)
Detail each area/room
Subdivide as needed
Make lists and tables
Make plans
Review and revise

See page 355 for the full process, and
page 349 for guidance on **running sites** .

**Encounters**

When the PCs cross paths with someone
or something that isn’t immediately hostile
or dangerous, that’s an encounter. This usually means people, but an encounter can be
with beasts, spirits, constructs, or anything
else the PCs can interact with.



**Building encounters**

Answer each of these questions, in whatever order makes sense. As the encounter
evolves in your mind, feel free to go back
and tweak or change earlier answers.



Who/what is the encounter with?
Where/when does it happen?
What are they doing?
Why are they here?
What do they want?
How do they react to the PCs?
Why are you putting this encounter in

the game? What’s at stake?



The more specific the answers, the more
interesting the encounter! “Migrants on
their way to Gordin’s Delve, in need of aid”
is a good encounter! But “A family from
Marshedge, going to join their father in
Gordin’s Delve, and whose donkey (and
all it carried) has wandered off the road” is
much more compelling.

If you’re improvising, you’ll answer many of
these questions instinctively, or already have
some answers from context. Great! Start with
what’s obvious, and add specific details as you
frame the encounter and play out the scene.

If you’re prepping, you might leave some
questions unanswered, or answer them
with possibilities instead of specifics. Even
answers you think are set in stone might
change in response to events in play.

**Running encounters**

Run encounters like any other part of the
game. Follow the **core loop** (page 169).
Frame the action with the encounter about
to start, or just starting, or already underway. Describe the environment and portray
your characters. Make a soft GM move,
ask what the PCs do, and resolve their
actions. Repeat until the encounter resolves
itself or becomes a new situation.

As you run the encounter, elaborate and
riff on what you’ve already established.
You’ll probably do this naturally as the
players ask questions and you clarify the
situation, or in order to resolve PC actions.

Build the NPCs into actual characters,
with names, core concepts, memorable
traits, impressions, and so forth. If the
encounter is with a group, embellish
specific individuals as they come into focus.
If you’re stumped for details, **ask the PCs**
**questions and build on the answers** .

If you’ve established the instinct, tags, and
moves of an NPC, use them to inform how
you portray them and run the scene.

Keep the encounter’s purpose and stakes
in mind. **Zoom to an appropriate level of**
**detail, and don’t be afraid to wrap a scene**
**up when its purpose has been fulfilled** .
For example, if you add an encounter with
the intent of just adding a little color, and
the PCs react as if it’s some sort of pivotal
scene, step out of character and address the
players. “Folks, no, this is just a tinker on
the road and not some sort of threat. Let’s
move on.” But at the same time, be open to
the possibility that this encounter is more
important or meaningful than you planned.
Maybe that tinker has some key information to share after all!

**Artifacts**

Artifacts are things laden with meaning,
power, or value. They’re objects that the
PCs might take with them, or come back
later to claim. Relics of the past, gems and
finery, magic items—treasure, basically!

_Book II_ contains over a hundred artifacts
that you can drop into your game. Some
are just brief descriptions and a few tags,
like “A makerglass jar with a threaded
lid, seals airtight ( _indestructible_, Value 2).”
Other artifacts have more formal writeups, often with custom moves. Like this:


**A large urn**
_Immobile, magical_
About 4 feet tall, made of thick shell-like
material, and fitted with a heavy lid. When
the **_urn is unoccupied and a Fae looks inside_**,
it is compelled to crawl in (it can always
fit). If you **_then close the lid_**, the Fae is
trapped and the urn slowly and painfully
peels away its knowledge, essence, and
sense of self. In time, nothing remains but
<u>a pile of pearls (Value 2).</u>


A handful of artifacts are presented as
**steading improvements** (page 514), and
many **arcana** (page 438) are some sort of
artifact. You can create your own, too; see
page 434.

If the PCs take an artifact with them,
make sure that someone adds it to their
inventory and is accounting for its load
(if any). If they bring it home, prompt
the players to decide whether it becomes
someone’s special possession or an asset of
the village as a whole.

Not all opportunities are equal. Sometimes
you’ll **offer an opportunity** directly, but
other times, you’ll merely **hint at more**
**than meets the eye** or **offer riches at a**
**price**, and the PCs have to work a bit to
take advantage of it. Some opportunities
will be exactly what the PCs need. Others
will be less obviously useful, or distractions
from their larger goals. And some are there
only because you think they should be, or
because a table in _Book II_ suggests it.

Once you put the opportunity in play, ask
the PCs what they do and go from there.
Often, they’ll jump on it, or attempt to.
But they might ignore it, or decide it’s not
worth the trouble, or pass it by for now
with plans to come back later. Don’t force
it. **Play to find out what happens!**

**Placing artifacts**

Put artifacts in the game where they make
sense. That sealed tomb that the PCs are
exploring probably has grave goods! The
sorcerer that they’re hunting probably has a
few magic artifacts at her disposal.

Put artifacts in the game, too, when you
think they’d be fun or interesting. Offer
one as a trade opportunity when Seasons
Change, or when a PC Trades & Barters.
Let a PC find one when they Forage and
choose to “discover something interesting
or valuable.”

When you prep a site, place artifacts in
specific spots and/or create a list of things
you can place when and wherever it makes
sense to do so.

As with any discovery, try to place artifacts
in a way that tells a story. Consider how it
came to be here, how long it’s been here,
how it’s shaped the environment or how
the environment has shaped it. Why has
no one claimed it? Who else is looking for
it? Who else knows it’s here? Reflect these
things in where you place an artifact and
how you describe it, as a way to **portray a**
**rich and mysterious world** .

**Identifying artifacts**

When the PCs find an artifact, describe it!
Tell them what they’ve found, and what’s
obvious at a glance.

If the artifact is **merely interesting, useful,**
**or valuable**, then make that clear as part
of your initial description or as soon as the
PCs interact with it. Don’t imply that there
might be more to discover if there really isn’t.


_“Each corpse has a bronze hatchet. The_
_heads are corroded and dull, but the_
_wooden handles have this beautiful_
_swirling grain. They’re remarkably well-_
_preserved, too. They don’t feel magical,_
_but the smith could restore the ax heads_
_with a little work, and they’d be, like,_
_<u>Value 1 each.”</u>_


**If there is more to the artifact**, then make
that clear as part of your description, or
**hint at more than meets the eye**, or just
**offer an opportunity** to learn more.


_The PCs dig the urn out of the rubble_
_and dust it off. “It’s quite large,” I tell_
_them. “Like four feet tall and yea big_
_around. The material is weird. It’s not_
_ceramic. More like… snail shell? And,_
_Blodwen, you in particular feel like it’s_
_<u>sort of… calling out? What do you do?”</u>_


From there, **follow the PCs’ actions and**
**resolve them** . If they look more closely,
tell them what they see. If they pick it up,
tell them what they feel. If the artifact has
a custom move, and they trigger it, then
present them with the move (and resolve
it, of course).

**If they Seek Insight** about the artifact,
resolve the move! On a 7+, answer their
question(s) honestly and helpfully, with as
much detail as the situation warrants.


_“What should you be on the lookout for?_
_Honestly, not much. The urn isn’t call-_
_ing you. It’s magic, yeah, but it doesn’t_
_<u>feel like magic that affects, like, mortals.”</u>_


**If they Know Things** about the artifact and
get a 7+, then tell them some combo of what
it is, what it does, what it’s worth, how they
might activate it or sell it for its full value, or
how they might learn more. If the artifact has
a custom move, then maybe you’d give them
some inkling of what it does on a 7-9, and
give them the move’s full text on a 10+.

Many of the artifacts in _Book II_ include
“something interesting” and “something
useful” as part of their description.

Ultimately, you want the players to know
what an artifact does and how to use it, so
that they can make meaningful decisions.
If the PCs can’t figure this out now, give
them a path toward doing so, such as…

… **telling them the requirements** . “Old

Gorlas knows about the Fae, he could
maybe tell you.” Or, “You’ll need to
bring it home and consult your scrolls.”

… **Making a Plan** (page 530) with them

to learn what it the artifact is or does.

… divulging the info via a **love letter**

(page 568).

… revealing the info after a bit of down
time. Maybe ask the player how the
PC learned it.

… some combination of the above.

Once a PC figures an artifact out, give the
player its tags, write-up, and any custom
moves, etc. If it’s a steading improvement,
add it to the steading playbook.

Arcana have their own considerations; see
page 438 for details.

**Artifacts in play**

Some artifacts are simply interesting or
valuable. But others have extraordinary
properties or generate magical effects.
When questions arise about an artifact
(like how it works, what’s involved with
using it, what it can/can’t do, etc.), **talk it**
**out with the players** . Consider the artifacts’
description, what’s implied about it, and
the specific details already established in
play. Extrapolate an answer together.

If the PCs want to leverage an artifact to
achieve some long-term goal, then Make a
Plan or write up a new steading improvement to reflect their idea, whichever makes
the most sense.

**Creating artifacts**

Appendix B ( _Book II_, page 498) includes a

**Arcana**

An arcanum is a card or playbook insert,
something you give the players. Each one
represents a discovery—an artifact, a place,
a bit of lore, a magical effect, etc.— with
mysteries that the PCs can unlock. “Arcanum” is a game term; it’s not something
characters are likely to refer to in-fiction.

The front of an arcanum describes what
the PC can tell at a glance or with a little
effort, plus the requirements for unlocking
its mysteries. The back shows those mysteries, which can include custom moves,
new options, followers, and consequences.

**Minor arcana** are small cards,
like the one shown here. They
provide no real benefit until
the PC unlocks them. Once
unlocked, the PC gains access
to all the benefits on the back.

﻿ **Major arcana** are half-page
playbook inserts; see page 61
for an example. Each offers
one or more “free” ﻿custom
moves. A PC who unlocks its
mysteries gains more powers,
but using those powers has
consequences.

_Stonetop_ includes 64 minor and
18 major arcana by default.
All of these are reproduced in
appendices c and d, starting on
page 508 of _Book II_ .

The Seeker playbook starts
with a major arcanum and two
minor arcana. The Heavy starts

Storm-marked background.

new arcana. Between unlocking them, using
them, and dealing with their consequences,

to your physique and health.”

**these requirements** . When there’s downtime, ask if they want to work on unlocking any arcana. If they’re unsure how to do

**Consequences**

Each major arcanum has a list of potential
consequences, with one or more checkboxes next to each one. Players mark a
consequence when one of the arcanum’s
moves says to, or when you tell them to do
so as a hard GM move.

The player decides which consequence to
mark. They don’t have to mark them in
order. “Nested” consequences should come
after the ones they’re nested beneath, but
aside from that, it’s the player’s choice.

Whichever consequence they mark, it happens. **Begin and end with the fiction** . Say
(or ask) how the consequence manifests,
what it looks like and feels like. **Bring it**
**home**, too; show how that consequence
affects others, and how NPCs react.

Consequences are a limited resource. They
stay marked even if their impact is passing,
temporary, or undone. If a PC marks, “You
are burned… Mark a debility and take
2d4 damage,” then that consequence stays
marked even after they regain their HP
and clear the debility.

Many consequences involve permanent
changes to the PC or the world. These
might be undone, but there’s no standard
way to do so. Make a Plan, based on the
established details in your game.

If all consequences have been marked, decide as a group whether that means there
are no more consequences to accrue (thus
making some moves impossible to trigger
and other moves safer to use), or if future
consequences mean that you make a hard
GM move, in line with the consequences
seen previously in play. The answer might
be different for each major arcanum, or
even for each move. Talk it out, together.

**Discoveries**



**NPCs using artifacts and arcana**



At some point, an NPC or monster will
get their hands on a magical artifact
or arcanum. The PCs might give it to
them, or teach them to use it or the
spell it imparts. Or, the NPC might
acquire/steal/borrow it from the PCs.
Or it might be in the NPC’s possession
to begin with.

Most magical artifacts and all arcana are
written to be player-facing, often with
custom moves or procedures that assume a PC is using them. So how does
that work when an NPC or monster is
the active party?

First, consider whether the NPC or
monster can use the artifact/arcanum at
all. Not everyone has the talent, learning, or aptitude to learn spells or command magical effects. Of course, some
magical artifacts just work and anyone
can use them.

If an antagonistic NPC or monster is
learning to use an artifact/arcanum, you
can reflect that as an impending doom
and grim portents, or even write up the
artifact/arcanum itself as a threat (a
_MacGuffin_, probably).

If one or more friendly NPCs are
learning to use an artifact or arcanum,
and that’s something the PCs want to
happen, then Make a Plan to lay out
the requirements. If their mastery of
this magic would provide some lasting
benefit to the village, then write it up as
a **steading improvement** (page 514).



To reflect a monster or NPC using
a magical artifact or arcanum, you
might…

… just **imagine how it works in their**

**hands**, and incorporate that into
your descriptions and GM moves
when you portray them;

… **reflect it in their stat block**, as one

or more tags, special qualities,
moves, or combinations thereof;

… **write custom player moves** (page

551) for what happens when they
use the artifact against the PCs,
and/or…

… **write it up as a threat** (probably an

_affliction_, a _magical entity_, or a
_MacGuffin_ ) if the NPC’s use of the
artifact is going to cause long-term
trouble. This is a particularly good
choice for major arcana—their consequences make great grim portents!

As you do all this, don’t worry about
replicating the artifact or arcanum
exactly. Magic is weird and fickle, and
might work differently for different
people or entities. Aim to capture the
feel of the requirements, moves, and
drawbacks, but don’t fret too much over
consistency.

**Creating**
**minor arcana**

Write up an artifact as a minor arcanum
when…

… you can envision a meaningful set of



steps for unlocking its mysteries
… once unlocked, it provides a lasting,



repeatable, and/or renewable benefit,
and…
… you think you can describe it and its



benefits concisely, in ~300 words or less
(~150 words per side).



**Initial title**
Write a short, simple phrase describing
what the arcanum appears to be. A minor
arcanum card can fit ~20 characters of
12-point type, so aim for that.

Examples: “A set of clay tablets,” or “A
broken glass sword.”

**Tags and others elements**
List the arcanum’s tags, load, and other
**game elements** . See page 437 for guidance.

**Brief description**
What is it? How do the PCs encounter it
and perceive it? How does it make them
feel? Keep it short, like 75 words or less,
but also make it as punchy as you can.

**Requirements**
Write a brief intro, like “To unlock the
mysteries of these tablets, you must…” Follow that with a series of checkbox steps.
The requirements might involve…

… deciphering/studying something;
… learning something, or figuring it out;
… finding/acquiring something;
… getting help from someone/thing;
… convincing someone/thing to __;
… days/weeks/months/seasons of effort;
… a certain skill or talent;
… making/repairing/finishing something;
… paying a cost/making a sacrifice;
… undergoing an ordeal/challenge;
… a ritual/rite/observance;
… something bloody/weird/extreme;
… labor/effort/practice/hard work;
… a simple custom move;
… risking __ (harm, collateral damage,

failing to master it, ruining it, etc.);
… combinations of the above; or…
… anything else that makes sense.



Good candidates include artifacts that
house a spirit or sentience, or that convey
the workings of a spell/ritual/technique, or
that produce a recurring magical effect.

Alternatively, you might have an idea for
some other discovery—a clue, a site, an
encounter—that meets the criteria above.
You might still walk through the artifact
creation process to conceptualize the idea,
but either skip the Form step or interpret it
very loosely.

With the arcanum’s concept in mind,
write the following, in whatever order
works for you:

Its initial title
Its tags and other elements
A brief description
The requirements for unlocking it
Its unlocked title
Its mysteries

Expect the process to be iterative. You’ll
often go back and revise earlier parts as
you work, maybe even rethinking its core
concept. That’s fine and normal.

List the requirements in the order that

**Mysteries**
Write out the back of the minor arcanum,

**Discoveries**


**Creating**
**major arcana**

A major arcanum is a half-page playbook
**insert** (page 57). Write up a discovery as
a major arcanum only if…



… you’re willing to do a fair amount of



work to create it;
… you’re okay with it being a significant



part of your ongoing game;
… you can see how it’d be immediately

useful (or at least interesting);
… you picture it growing in power with



effort or as the PC uses it; and…
… you can imagine an escalating series of



consequences from using it.



Start with the arcanum’s concept—its core
themes, what it is, and what it does. Like,
“a Fae-made mirror that creates resentful
copies of yourself,” or, “a shard of divine
essence that burns away your flesh and
replaces it with holy flame.” If you haven’t
already, use the **creating artifacts** process
(page 434) to help conceptualize it, but
don’t feel constrained by that process.

Each major arcanum works differently, but
almost all have these common elements.
Flesh them out in whatever order works
for you:

Its title, tags, and a brief description
Its starting moves
How it’s unlocked
Its mysteries
Its consequences

Refer to the existing major arcana for a
sense of structure and what’s possible. See
**Noruba’s Ice Sphere** (page 61) for one
example, and Appendix D ( _Book II_, page
540) for the rest

**Title, tags, and description**
Give your major arcanum a Proper Title,
something capitalized, evocative, maybe
a little ominous. The “Misty Mirror” and
“The Everflame” are good examples.

List its tags and other **game elements**
(page 437), including its load (if any).

Write a brief description of what it is and
how it appears to the PCs. Make it as clear
and punchy as you can, hinting at the arcanum’s power. But also, keep it short and
concise. Aim for 75 words or less.

Draw it or find a picture to represent it,
something to place at the top of the insert.

**Starting moves**
Write the starting move(s), the ones that
are easily accessible to a PC who acquires
the arcanum. These should be useful and
compelling, something the player(s) will be
excited (or at least tempted) to use.

**How it’s unlocked**
Write the requirements as a distinct move,
as a checklist, or as part of the arcanum’s
initial move(s). These requirements might
involve…

… successfully using one of the arcanum’s

starting moves __ times;
… rolling a 6- on one of the initial moves



__ times (suffering the consequences
each time);
… paying a certain cost __ times;
… collecting specific items or bits of lore;
… performing certain tasks or rituals;
… spending weeks practicing/studying/



contemplating the arcanum;
… experiencing some extreme event(s);
… some combination of the above; or
… anything else that makes sense.

followers that a PC can gain by unlocking
the arcanum. They should be quite potent,

Keep the mysteries as concise as you can.
They need to share one side of a half-page
insert with the consequences; you’ve got
~200 words to work with, 300 at most.

**Consequences**
Create 5-10 consequences that a PC might
incur when they use the arcanum’s mysteries. If you write 6 or fewer, give some of
them multiple checkboxes to indicate that
they can be suffered multiple times. A major
arcanum should have ~8 checkboxes total.

Make 2-3 of the consequences not-so-bad,
maybe even beneficial. But by the time the
player is marking the fourth or fifth one,
things should be feeling grim.



resistance to certain harms, etc.;
… changes to how others perceive the PC
… introducing a new threat or danger
… unwanted attention;
… new, problematic custom moves;
… a task the PC must perform;
… chaos wrought upon the larger world;
… the loss of the arcanum or its powers;
… some combination of the above; or…

It can help to think of a major arcanum as
a **threat** (page 277), with the consequences acting as grim portents and impending
dooms that the player gets (has) to pick.

**Edit and revise**
Look at what you’ve got so far, and revise
mercilessly. The arcanum’s starting moves,
its mysteries and the way they’re unlocked,
and its consequences should all reflect its
concept and themes. They should suggest
a story, but not dictate one. The pieces
should all work together, tempting both
player and PC to keep using the arcanum,
despite the inevitable costs.

Write multiple drafts. Edit heavily. Be
willing to discard ideas that you love. Share
drafts with your players and other GMs.
Be open to feedback. Get it as good as you
can before putting it in play, but be willing
to revise if something doesn’t work.

Writing a major arcanum is a lot of work,
but it can be very rewarding.
