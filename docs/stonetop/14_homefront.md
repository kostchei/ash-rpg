**Homefront**


The **homefront** is what happens before,
after, and between adventures. It’s what
happens in the village of Stonetop and its
immediate surroundings. It’s where you get
to see the PCs living their lives, and the
impact of the PCs’ efforts.

Homefront play is often less focused and
less intense than what happens while the
PCs are on an expedition. You spend a lot
more time in **loose play** (page 176), talking about what happens over days or weeks
or months rather than what’s happening
_right here_ and _right now_ . And when you do
zoom in on a homefront scene, the stakes
are often quite small.
That’s not to say the homefront doesn’t
matter. To the contrary, **the homefront**
**is the heart of the game** . PCs in _Stonetop_
undertake adventures on behalf of the
village. They’re protecting it from threats
or securing resources for the folks who live
there. The homefront provides context for
everything the PCs do.

For the game to work, **the players need to**
**care about the village** . Not just the PCs,
the _players_ . That means spending tabletime getting to know it, getting invested
in it. Let the players help you shape the
village. Work with them to bring it to life.
Let them see how it changes with the
passing of time. Show them how their decisions and actions matter, how they shape
the village for better or for worse.

**Bringing**
**the village**
**to life**

If you want the PCs to care about the
village, it needs to feel real. It needs to
feel _alive_ .

Following your **principles** (page 192)
will help you bring the village to life. As a
reminder, they are:

Follow the rules
Begin and end with the fiction
Address the characters, not the players
Ask questions and build on the answers
Be a fan of the player characters
Embrace the fantastic and the mundane
Exploit the setting guide
Respect your prep
Give your characters life
Think offscreen, too
Bring it home
Let things breathe
Let things burn

**Follow the rules**
Player moves—especially the **homefront**
**player moves** (page 524)—will introduce
unexpected opportunities, challenges,
and turns of events. Let those surprises
illuminate the village and make it feel real.
Life is full of surprises. Your version of
Stonetop should be, too!

**Begin and end with the fiction**
When players trigger a homefront move,
ask how they do it. They Requisition the
horses? Cool, who do they talk to and
what’s their pitch? They Muster the village?
What does it look like? A town meeting? A
speech at the public house? Or what?




--- end of page=483 ---

Show the results of player moves in your
descriptions, and in your portrayal of
NPCs. The PCs Requisition the horses?
Then describe folks hauling a cart themselves, glaring as the PCs leave town. A
PC visits her sister after a Muster? Then
portray the sister as tired and frazzled, and
say how her shield and spear are by the
door instead of hanging on the wall.

When the steading playbook changes, reflect
those changes in the fiction, too. If Fortunes
go up, describe the town’s good mood. If you
mark _diminished_, show how work isn’t getting
done. If Population goes up, discuss how the
newcomers are settling in.

Show players how the game’s mechanics
change the village, and the village will feel
dynamic and alive. Show players how their
moves and their decisions affect the village,
and they’ll start to feel responsible for it.

**Address the characters,**
**not the players**
Describe things from the characters’ points
of view. Direct questions to the characters
rather than the players. Don’t tell Brian
that folks are scared of Vahid; say, “Vahid,
you can tell you’re making folks nervous—
how so?” Focus on how the PCs experience
the village, and the village will seem that
much more alive.

**Ask questions and**
**build on the answers**
Whenever you wonder something about
the village or about daily life, consider asking the PCs about it.

**Ask what they’re up to** as you frame the
action. “Caradoc, what task or chore are
you working on?”



**Homefront**


**Ask for details**, to help paint the scene.
“What’s cooking on the hearth?” “Vahid,
what makes this place feel like home?”

**Ask them to editorialize.** “What’s the
worst part of this chore?” “What do you
love most about Stonetop in spring?”

**Ask about their lives.** “Rhianna, what’s
your morning routine like?” “Do you usually eat alone, or what?”

**Ask about NPCs.** “Blodwen, who taught
you how to weave?” “Rhianna, who do the
farmers typically listen to?”

**Ask about traditions and practices.** “Caradoc, how does the village mark one’s coming
of age?” “Blodwen, how does Stonetop mark
or celebrate the coming of winter?”

**Ask about gossip, stories, and lore.** “Vahid, what do folks say about Gorlas behind
his back?” “Rhianna, when did someone
last break the compact and fell a tree in the
Wood? What happened?”

When a player asks something about the
village, maybe turn the question back on
them or direct it to another PC. “I don’t
know! Caradoc, given the lack of timber,
what _do_ people use for doors?”

**Build on their answers, now or later.** Ask
follow-up questions. Treat their answers
as if they’ve always been true. Call back to
them in later scenes. Show the players that
their answers matter, that they shape the
village, and you’ll find that they can’t help
but care.

**Homefront**


**Be a fan of the player characters**
If you want the players to care about the
village, then you need to care about their
characters, about their personal lives and
their relationships. Get invested in the
Fox’s secret crush, the Heavy’s fraught
home life, the Seeker’s web of lies. Put the
PCs’ dramas on screen, and **play to find**
**out what happens** .

**Embrace the fantastic**
**and the mundane**
Spend time establishing what life in
Stonetop is like. **Talk about the weird**
**stuff** : the quiet power of the Stone, the
faded grandeur of the Maker-ruins, the
gloomy menace of the Great Wood. But
**talk about mundane stuff**, too! Crops.
Weather. Livestock. Food and clothes.
Chores, crafts, and pastimes.

**Talk about the village’s culture and social**
**fabric** . The game asserts that Stonetop is
egalitarian, that everyone contributes and
everyone shares. But what that looks like is
up to your group. So are all sorts of details
about village life. Kinship. Child care.
Gender roles. Institutions and traditions.
Holidays and celebrations. Myths and
legends. Etc.

There’s a balance to strike, of course. Focus
on stuff your group cares about. But don’t
assume that mundane stuff is unimportant.
**Portray a** **_rich_** **and mysterious world**, not
just a mysterious one.


**486**



**Exploit the setting guide**
The Setting Overview handout and the
**Village of Stonetop** entry ( _Book II_, page
12) provide plenty of details that can help
bring the community to life.

When you frame a homefront scene, skim
**the Impressions section** . Pick one or two
and incorporate them into the scene. It’s
spring? Set the scene in the fields, planting
beans, the PCs coated in cold wet mud. A
PC visits their sibling? Mention the smell
of fat rendering on the hearth, the rushlights hanging up to dry. Two PCs want a
private moment? Frame a scene at night,
with one of them manning a watchtower
and the other one stopping by.

**Extrapolate from what’s established**,
especially with things that strike you and
the players as odd or anachronistic. The villagers don’t fell living trees from the Great
Wood. How does that affect construction
and tools? Do houses have doors? Where
do folks get wood for spears, bows, and
arrows? They can’t _fell_ trees, but can they
coppice them? Decide for yourself or (better yet) ask the PCs!

**It’s okay to change or ignore things** . _Ex-_
_ploit_ the setting guide, don’t feel constrained
by it. With that said, think about what
you’re changing and what ripple effects that
change might have. Little details (like swapping Stonetop’s whisky for mead) are safer
to change than core assumptions (like the
village’s egalitarian culture).

**Handling**
**misconceptions**

Elements of _Stonetop_ ’s setting can be
counterintuitive for players steeped in
the tropes of more traditional fantasy
RPGs. For example, you might find
your players assuming that…

... locals pay each other with coin

(coin is for outsiders; locals swap
goods and favors, give gifts, owe
each other, help each other out).

... folks need jobs to get by (you’re

expected to pull your weight, but
folks help each other out; no one
starves unless all are starving).

... the village boasts a variety of

trades (there’s only a few fulltime tradesfolk; most crafts are
done by homemakers or other
folk as they have time).

... there are shops (nope), a mill

(nuh-uh), an inn (no), multiple
taverns (heck no), etc.

Watch out for such misconceptions.
If a player mentions “needing coin,”
or says they run a shop, or otherwise
reveals a disconnect between their
assumptions and the default setting,
then call attention to it. Don’t just tell
them they’re wrong, talk it out!

“So, there aren’t shops in Stonetop.
Like, folks come to you if they want
salt or spices, but I think they’d just
come by your house. We can say it’s
set up like a shop, if you like, but few
folks here know what a shop is.”



**Homefront**


**Respect your prep**
In particular: write up **threats** (page
282) and then use them in play. The
town busybody might be an _institution_ .
The bright kid who idolizes the Seeker?
A _wildcard_ . The Stone itself? Probably a
_MacGuffin_ . Make binding decisions about
these threats. Write impending dooms that
involve the village and leave lasting marks
upon it. Write stakes that involve NPCs
that you and the players care about. And
then put those threats on screen. See how
the PCs react to them. Follow through.

**Give your characters life**
Populate the village with NPCs who are
distinct and memorable, believable and
compelling (see **Creating NPCs** for guidance, page 453). Keep track of the NPCs
you establish, and bring them back into play
over time. Show how they change and grow,
how they’re affected by the PCs’ actions.

The PCs don’t have to like or get along
with everyone in the village. Some of
the villagers should be threats! But give
troublesome neighbors some nuance, some
reason the PCs can’t just ignore them or
run them out of town, such as:

A sympathetic motivation
A redeeming quality
A skill that the village needs
A family member who loves them
A position of leadership/influence
The respect/loyalty of other NPCs
Something to hold over a PC (an oath,

a secret, a familial bond, etc.)

**Homefront**


**Think offscreen, too**
When the PCs head home at the end of an
expedition, think about what’s been happening back in the village. What have folks
been up to? What’s morale been like? What
rumors and gossip have spread? What
threats have escalated in the PCs’ absence?
What projects have moved forward?

When the PCs arrive home, show the players what’s been happening. Describe the
tension in the watch-sentry’s voice as the
PCs approach, the haggard look in people’s
eyes. Mention how the fields have all been
planted and now they’re being harrowed.
Say how Macsen’s health has worsened, or
how the palisade is coming together.

Show players that the village changes
while the PCs aren’t around, and it will feel
much more real, much more alive.
**Bring it home**
Make the village the foundation of the
game. Get the players to help create and
define the village and the NPCs who live
there. Give them a chance to care. Then
threaten the village, offer opportunities
to make things better, and **show how the**
**PCs’ decisions and efforts change the vil-**
**lage**, for good or for ill.

With that said: don’t just show how the PCs
are important to the village. **Show how the**
**village is important to the PCs** . Explore relationships between the PCs and the NPCs
they care about. Have those NPCs support
and care for the PCs. Have NPCs thank the
PCs for their efforts. Have NPCs apologize
for falling short, and try to do better.

Make it clear that the village of Stonetop
isn’t just a home base, it’s _home_ . And make
it a home worth protecting, a home worth
fighting for.


**488**



**Let things breathe**
Your job is to **_punctuate_** **the PCs lives with**
**adventure** . There should be stretches of
time in which very little happens. Gloss
over days, weeks, or months. Let the PCs
work on long-term projects or just live
their lives. Maybe a season passes with no
more than some description and a little
Q&A. Stonetop comes to life over time, as
the seasons change and characters age and
long-term mechanics start to kick in.

Your players might fight this, wittingly or
not. They might want to set right out on
another adventure, or relentlessly work
on some mystery or problem. If you aren’t
careful, you’ll spend dozens of sessions on
a single season.

Don’t be afraid to zoom out, **remind them**
**of their obligations**, and show the passing
of time. If you want to get to the next
season, don’t ask what they do for the rest
of the summer. Instead say, “The next few
weeks pass uneventfully, until the days
get shorter and the leaves start to change.
What chores have eaten up your time these
past few weeks?”

**Let things burn**
If you do your job, you’ll end up with a village and a bunch of NPCs that the players
care about. That you care about.

Do not protect them.

You have to be willing to let bad things
happen to the village and to beloved
NPCs. Stay within the boundaries of your
group’s **content guidelines** (page 66),
but otherwise: take the gloves off. If the
stakes aren’t real, then the PCs’ heroism
isn’t real, either.

**Running**
**the homefront**

Homefront play is very loose. The PCs
are often separated from each other, living
their own lives and doing their own things.
You need to decide what to gloss over vs.
what scenes to play out.

**Before the first adventure**, you’ll frame
scenes that establish life in the village.
You’ll then drop the hook for the adventure, and follow the PCs around as they
react to it, make preparations for the
expedition, and head out.

In the **aftermath of an adventure**, you’ll
shift back into homefront play. You’ll show
how the villagers react to recent events and
the PCs return. You’ll give each PC a
chance to process the adventure and
reconnect with NPCs. You’ll play out
scenes that naturally follow, and then
transition to downtime.

**During downtime**, you’ll zoom way out.
You’ll describe time passing and drop into
scenes only as needed. The PCs will make
homefront moves like Bolster, Convalesce,
or Level Up. Maybe they’ll work on longterm projects or Make a Plan to do so. If
enough time passes, the seasons will change.

Eventually, something will spur **the next**
**adventure** . The PCs will identify something they want to do out in the world,
or you’ll introduce a threat that they feel
compelled to deal with.

Throughout it all, you’ll follow the **core**
**loop** (page 169) and make GM moves—
especially **homefront moves** (page 502).



**Homefront**


**Before the first adventure**

Start the first session of actual play at
home in the village. Frame mundane
scenes that paint a picture of each PC’s
daily life. Explore the PCs’ relationships,
with each other and with important NPCs.
Use these scenes to establish recent events
and current concerns. Play out some daily
dramas, but don’t, like, _resolve_ them.

Then, **drop the hook** for your adventure.
Follow the PCs around as they decide
what to do about it. Figure out what’s
important to them, and push them out into
the field. Portray how NPCs react to both
the hook and the PCs’ plans.

The whole **First Adventure** chapter (page
251) sets you up to do this. Follow the
procedures laid out in that chapter!

**Homefront**


**Aftermath**

After each expedition, take time to:

**1)** Determine what’s happened
**2)** Play out the return
**3)** See what follows

Don’t skip this part! The PCs have been
roughing it and putting themselves in
danger on the village’s behalf. Use their
homecoming to demonstrate that their
efforts matter, and to remind them of who
and what they’ve been fighting for. **Bring**
**it home** .

The same structure applies after a **crisis**
(page 499) at home abates. Figure out
what happened and who was affected, play
out an initial post-crisis scene, and then see
what scenes naturally follow.

**what’s happened**

minutes to consider how things have been
going while the PCs were away. Think
about the following.

**How long were the PCs gone?** What have
folks been up to or dealing with? How’s
morale been? Who’s been worried sick, or
resentful, or stepping up while the PCs were
away? If the PCs were gone longer than
expected, how did folks react to their delay?

**If anyone on the expedition was wounded**
**or didn’t make it home**, who will be most
upset or affected by that?

affect people’s lives (if at all)?

**2** <sup>**Play out the return**</sup>
Frame the action with the PCs
arriving back at the village, or at least on
their last leg of travel. Say what time it is,
what direction they’re approaching from,
what’s obvious as they draw near.

Ask questions! What are they each thinking
about or worrying about or looking forward
to? Do they intend to keep any secrets about
what happened on the expedition, or spin
things in any way? Are they just trudging
home, or putting on a bit of a show, or what?

Maybe have them meet an NPC before
reaching the village proper. Such an encounter can help ease you all back into
homefront play, and foreshadow the village’s reaction to the PCs’ return.


_“It’s early evening, four days after you_
_left. The sun’s low, casting long shadows_

When the PCs get back to town, use the
**impressions** of Stonetop ( _Book II_, page 14)
to set the scene. **Think offscreen, too.** Include details that point to what’s happened
while the PCs were away.

**If the PCs are Returning Triumphant**
(page 339), then launch into what that
looks like. Describe the news spreading, a
crowd gathering, toasts, celebration! But
also: say who hangs back, glaring, grumbling, still unhappy or just left out.

If the PCs’ return would cause panic or
reveal a calamity (for example, if they took
the village’s horses and lost them), **then the**
**village Meets with Disaster** (page 532).
Describe folks gathering to hear the
bad news, their fearful eyes, the worried
mumbles or the wails of grief. But also: say
who steps up, who supports the PCs, who
tries to talk sense.

If the PCs return _could_ be triumphant, or
_could_ cause panic, but it’s not a given either
way, then set it up as scene. Frame the action, portray your NPCs, and make a GM
move. Maybe **tell them the requirements**
(“You’re all gonna need to keep your stories
straight to make this Return Triumphant”)
or **put them in a spot** (“Folks are grumbling,
on the verge of panic”). Ask, “What do you
do?” and see if the PCs can pull it off.

**Otherwise**, just describe their return home
and how folks react, however it makes
sense to do so. Ask questions as needed
and build on the answers.

From there, ask, **“What do you do?”** Play
out the situation until all the PCs have
gone their separate ways.

ways, frame a few more scenes and situations that play out over the next few days,
before you transition to downtime. In

Actively manage the spotlight, hopping

to do things roughly in order, but don’t get

**The next adventure**

Downtime ends with the next adventure,
which might be an expedition or a crisis, and
either spontaneous or prepped in advance.

**Expeditions**
**Expeditions** (page 301) involve the PCs
making preparations, traveling towards a
destination, having encounters, facing challenges, and eventually going home.

The PCs can always choose to launch an
expedition, but they’ll most often do so
in response to a hook—some threat, opportunity, or requirement that you present
to them, as the result of a player move or
just because that’s the GM move you chose
to make.

Should the PCs propose an ill-conceived
expedition, then push back. **Remind them**
**of their obligations** . They have chores to
do and obligations around town. **Tell them**
**consequences/requirements.** Encourage
them to wait for a hook or to seek one out.

**Crises**
A **crisis** means active, urgent badness
in the village, here and now. A monster
attacks. A house catches fire. Someone is
murdered. Whatever it is, it’s bad and it
has the potential to get worse.

A crisis is usually a hard GM move, something you do when a player rolls a 6- or
when the PCs ignore trouble. But a few
player moves can generate crises even on a
7+. For example, when the Seasons Change
to spring or autumn, a 7-9 says that “a
threat to the steading makes itself known or
gets worse,” which could manifest as a crisis.

Resolve a crisis like any situation. Frame
the action. Describe the environment. Add
details as needed. Ask and answer questions. Portray NPCs. Make a GM move to
put the crisis on screen. Ask, “What do you
do?” Resolve their actions. Repeat. **Play to**
**find out what happens.**

The village might Meet With Disaster.
Maybe the PCs prevent calamity and
panic, maybe they don’t. It might not even
be possible. If the crisis starts with two
farmers slaughtered in the fields, then
calamity has already struck.

After a crisis, the PCs might immediately
launch an expedition in response (per the
previous page).

If they don’t head out immediately, shift
back to homefront play and deal with the
**aftermath** (page 490). Figure out what
happened, who was affected, and how.
Play out a scene immediately following
the crisis as folks take stock. Play out some
follow-up scenes, including a scene with
each PC and their family or other close
NPCs. Then transition back to downtime.

**Spontaneous adventures**
An adventure might start mid-session,
with no chance for you to prep. This can
happen when…



... the players launch an expedition on



their own initiative (to pursue a
steading improvement, to unlock an
arcanum, to investigate a site, etc.);
... a player’s move prompts you to present

a threat, an opportunity, or a requirement, and you present one that spurs
an expedition;
... it’s time to make a GM move, and you

**announce trouble** or **offer an oppor-**
**tunity** or otherwise drop a hook, and
the PCs act on it; or
... a player rolls a 6- or knowingly invites



trouble, and your hard GM move
manifests as a crisis or kicks off an
expedition.



Don’t panic. **Run the game.** Frame
scenes. Manage the spotlight. Follow the
core loop. Zoom out into loose play and
identify the next scene or situation. **Ask**
**questions and build on the answers.** Take
a quick break if needed to collect your
thoughts. **Respect your prep**, especially
your threats. **Exploit the setting guide**, use
it to improvise.

If you and the players want to cut to the
action, then frame the next scene with
PCs _already on the adventure_ . Then, ask
questions to establish what led them here
and what’s already happened. Have them
Outfit retroactively, and go!

If a spontaneous adventure doesn’t wrap
up by the session’s end, cool. Now you have
time to prep! See “ **Between sessions”** on
page 575).

**Prepared adventures**
If you expect to start an adventure in the
next (or an upcoming) session, then spend
some time prepping it.

Like, if the players told you that they
want to go on an expedition, then Chart a
Course, pick out points of interest and legs

opportunity, figure out how you’ll present
it to the players, and prep the expedition
itself. If you’re going to drop a crisis, then

who’ll be in its way, etc. See **Between ses-**
**sions** (page 575) for guidance.

session, too. Do you want to kick things
off with some pointed questions or **love**
**letters** (page 568)? Are there any scenes
or situations you want to play out before
diving into the action?

**Fortunes**
The **Fortunes** stat reflects the morale and
social cohesion of the village, the favor of
the gods, and just “how things are going.”
Fortunes start at +1, and can range from
-1 to +3.

When Fortunes are high, people are (generally) happy and generous with each other.
The future seems bright.
When Fortunes are low, people are (generally) grumpy or despairing. They talk of
dark times ahead. They start looking to
themselves and their own rather than the
community at large.

Fortunes go up when the PCs **Return**
**Triumphant** (page 339) or complete
certain improvements (page 514). They
go down when the village **Meets with**
**Disaster** (page 532). Fortunes might go
down when the PCs **Muster** (page 534),
**Requisition** (page 308), or have the village **Pull Together** (page 536).

Players roll +Fortunes to **Requisition**
(page 308). More importantly, they roll
+Fortunes when the **Seasons Change**
(page 517) to see what the season has in
store for the village and the PCs.

Fortunes reset to +1 (or +0 if the steading
is _malcontent_ ). This means that changes to
Fortunes themselves are passing, applying
to only the next season.

**Surplus**
**Surplus** reflects stores of food and trade
goods. It represents crops in the granary
and in household larders, livestock ready
for slaughter, whisky that’s ready to drink,
fur that’s ready to trade or be fashioned
into garments.

Surplus isn’t a stat that’s rolled; it’s a
resource that Stonetop accumulates, consumes, and spends. The village starts with 1
Surplus; there’s no upper limit.

The steading generates Surplus each summer and autumn, and consumes Surplus
each winter. PCs can gain more Surplus
through trade, by going on an expedition
to hunt big game, or as a **seasonal gain**
(page 518). The PCs might spend Surplus
to trade for other assets or to have folks
**Pull Together** (page 536) on a project.

Should the PCs wish to Trade & Barter
with or for Surplus, 1 Surplus is Value 2
(see **Value and coins**, page 542).

Each steading has a **Size** tag, which describes the scale of its infrastructure: its

**ter** (see page 518), as well as the **Muster**
(page 534), **Pull Together** (page 536),

**_Hamlet_** (<50 people): Consume 1d2

**_Village_** (150-350 people): Consume 1d4

+Population Surplus each winter

**Defenses**
**Defenses** represent the martial prowess and
readiness of the steading: how well-trained
and well-armed the residents are, how

When Defenses are low, there are few
competent warriors in the steading. Arms
and armor are limited and/or in poor
repair. Folks tend to panic, and are easy to
catch off guard.

The steading playbook includes a number

**Requirements**
The requirements for each improvement

band of Hillfolk”) to the seemingly impossible (“Some method of making water flow
uphill”). If the players don’t know how
to go about meeting such a requirement,
**Make a Plan** (page 530) with them.

The PCs can complete requirements for
an improvement in whatever order makes
sense; they don’t have to go in order unless
an improvement specifically says they do.

Some improvements require individuals
with specific skills, like “an _exceptional_ engineer/foreman” or “a veteran warrior, able
to command a crowd.” This might be a PC,
or an NPC who already lives in Stonetop.
Otherwise, they’ll either need to recruit
someone from abroad or wait for such a
person to move to town.

When an improvement requires Surplus
or other materiel, it’s used up or otherwise committed to the improvement. The
Palisade improvement requires “lots of
timber… (Value 3).” You can’t use that timber for other projects without dismantling
some or all of the palisade!

Many improvements involve **Pulling**
**Together** one or more times. See page
536 for details.

Once the PCs meet all the requirements

mostly mechanical, but don’t forget about
the fictional impact! If the PCs unlock
the Aurochs Hunting improvement by

they’ve improved their relationship with

Once an improvement is completed,

they lose the improvement. Make a Plan

**Homefront**


**Seasons Change**

The Seasons Change move creates the
long-term rhythm of the game. It breaks
up downtime. It requires the PCs to care
about the steading’s **Fortunes** (page 508)
and **Surplus** (page 509). It makes many
of the steading improvements important
and valuable to pursue.

**Making the move**
The Seasons Change during downtime.
When you’ve played out any activities,
scenes, and situations you and the players
are interested in, skip ahead in time and
re-solve this move.

Aim to resolve the move towards the end
of a session if you can, so that you can prep
the next session based on the results. If you
can’t, cool, just be willing to improvise!

**Rolling**
Each season indicates who rolls for the
move (whoever is most hopeful rolls in
spring, whoever is most content rolls in
summer, etc.). “Whoever” refers to the PC,
not the player. If you really can’t decide,
pick randomly.

Whoever rolls also makes any choices required by the move. They can ask for input,
but the final decisions are theirs.

Each season involves rolling +Fortunes,
which results in some combination of
seasonal gains, threats, and (in winter) the
need to consume additional Surplus. More
on that later.

After the roll, reset Fortunes to +1 (or +0 if
the village is currently _malcontent_ ).


**516**



**Generating Surplus**
In summer, the village generates 1d4-1
Surplus (from pickled spring vegetables,
surplus winter potatoes, summer fruits,
whisky maturing, game, etc.). If they also
get a seasonal gain, the player can wait to
pick it until they see how much Surplus
they generate.

When the autumn harvest is complete, the
village gets another 1d4 Surplus (crops,
garden produce, game, mature livestock,
etc.). Harvest is a process that takes most
of autumn. If something significantly
disrupts it, have the player roll for Surplus
with disadvantage and/or a -1 penalty. If
the harvest fails entirely, they don’t get any
Surplus (and they Meet With Disaster).

**Improving the rolls**
PCs cannot Aid each other on either
the +Fortunes roll or on rolls to generate
Surplus. The move assumes that everyone
is doing their part already.

PCs _can_ Bolster for a coming season and
spend the Preparation on the roll(s), but
doing so should requiring a significant sacrifice to the gods (like 1 Surplus at least),
and/or having the village Pull Together to

**BOLSTER**
When you **_prepare for what’s coming_**
**_or seek the favor of the gods_**, say how
and answer the GM’s questions.
Then, hold Preparation based on the
amount of time you devote:

A week or so: 1 Preparation
A month or so: 2 Preparation
The better part of a season:

3 Preparation

When you **_make any roll to which your_**
**_efforts might apply_**, you may spend 1
Preparation to add +1 to that roll, after it is made (maximum +1 per roll).


This move happens during downtime,
when a PC dedicates time and effort to
training or study, to the making of plans,
or to religious ceremony or offerings, in
preparation for some future challenge or
undertaking.

The times listed assume that the PC is
devoting themselves to Bolstering, to the
exclusion of other projects, expeditions, or
even day-to-day responsibilities. It should
be noticeable and kind of a big deal. You
can let them Bolster part time, if you want,
but it should then take the better part of a
season to gain just 1 Preparation.


**524**



Time spent Bolstering doesn’t count towards other projects, and vice versa.
When the Marshal runs drills to achieve
the Well-trained Militia improvement,
they’re Pulling Together, not Bolstering.
But if the steading already has the Welltrained Militia improvement, and the Marshal spends an extra week drilling folks on
a tactic they’ve already learned, then they’re
Bolstering.

The player has to describe how they’re
Bolstering. Clarify exactly what they’re
preparing for. If you think their efforts are
unequal to the task, push back and **tell**
**them the requirements** . A week spent
seeking out tales from the elders might be
a good way to Bolster for a trip among the
Fae, but it won’t help them improve the
autumn harvest.

Large-scale preparations require more
than just the PCs’ time and efforts. If the
PCs want to Bolster for a coming Seasons
Change roll or an upcoming battle, they
need to involve the village as a whole. They
might need to Persuade key NPCs, Pull
Together, and/or spend 1 or more Surplus
or similar resources.

Once you and the player have settled
on how they’re Bolstering and for what,
discuss the impacts of doing so. How long
do they plan to spend? What work goes
undone? Who do they ask/expect to cover
for them?

Frame a scene or two with the PC while
they’re Bolstering, to **bring it home** and
show how their choices impact their house,
their family, their neighbors, etc. The more
time the PC spends, and the more folks
they involve, the bigger a deal it should be.

rolls for moves, but they can spend it on
any damage rolls, rolls for Surplus, or even

**526**



Deploy only triggers if the PCs take
charge. When villagers go into danger on
their own initiative, or come under attack
with no PC to rally them, the move doesn’t
trigger—you decide what happens, based
on your principles, your prep, and the
established fiction.

Before the player rolls, say what a 10+
looks like. Does it mean winning the
battle, or pulling off a single desperate

solid fictional positioning: the high ground,
superior numbers, surprise, magic, etc.

village has trained (per the Well-trained

**LEVEL UP**
When you **_have a quiet stretch of time_**
**_at home and XP equal to (or greater_**
**_than) 6 + twice your current level_**,
follow these steps:

1) Subtract 6 + twice your current
level from your XP.
2) Increase your level by 1.
3) Choose a new move from your
class playbook, or an insert that
you’ve unlocked.
4) If you are the Blessed (or have a
sacred pouch) and your new level is
even, increase your max Stock by 1.
5) If you are the Lightbearer (or
have Invoke the Sun God) and
your new level is even, choose a
new invocation.
6) Review your Instinct and Appearance (on the front of your playbook). Change anything that no
longer applies. Feel free to make
up new options.


PCs can’t Level Up “in the field.” They
need to head home. If they accumulate
more XP than they need to Level Up,
encourage them to **Burn Brightly** (page
231) whenever they can—the rest of their
expedition will go more smoothly.

“A quiet stretch of time” usually means a
few days with no crises or adventures or
significant risks. But don’t be a stickler; if
everyone has enough XP and they get just
one quiet night before heading back out,
let them Level Up. And if a session ends at
home, any PC who gets enough XP from
the End of Session move should Level Up
between sessions, even if the session ended
on a cliffhanger.


**528**



If a PC has enough XP to Level Up twice,
then they Level Up twice right away. No
need to wait for another quiet stretch of time.

Like any move, if you do it, you have to do
it. Players can’t delay Leveling Up in order
to, say, Burn Brightly on a Seasons Change
roll. But with that said, players will sometimes need to ponder their choice of new
moves. That’s fine! They can’t delay Leveling
Up to game the system, but they can take
time to think. Encourage them to pick before the next session. But if they’re stumped,
they can pick a new move during play.

Players can pick any move from their
playbook whose requirements they meet.
They don’t need to ask permission or justify
their choice. If their pick asserts a fictional
change (like the Seeker taking Initiate of
Secret Arts and getting a sacred pouch),
ask the player what that looks like and how
that happened—not as a challenge, but to
clarify the fiction.

If a player wants a move from another
playbook, but doesn’t have an option that
explicitly allows it, they’re welcome to
pitch the idea to the table. If everyone
(not just you) agrees, okay. But consider
whether the choice makes sense for their
character, whether it creates a weird rules
interaction, whether it infringes on another
PC’s niche, etc.

A move that requires a specific playbook is
never available to other playbooks. No one
but the Heavy can take Dangerous, and
no one but the Would-be Hero can have
Potential for Greatness.

Players can take a move that requires a
certain level as they achieve that level. For
example, they can take Superior Stat when
they Level Up to level 6.

If a move _replaces_ a different move, then it
requires the one it replaces. If a player takes
such a move, they lose the original move
and any benefits it conferred.

There’s no maximum level, but you
might find that players start to run out of
playbook moves that they’re interested in
taking. If that happens, encourage them to
browse unused playbooks for moves that
might make sense, and have them pitch
them to the group. Or, if you have the time
and inclination, create a playbook **insert**

potential playbook moves.

The last step is for players to review their
Instinct and Appearance, and to change
them if they no longer feel right. To be
clear, players can change these things at
pretty much any time it makes sense for
them to do so. The Level Up move just
provides a formal reminder to consider
doing so.
